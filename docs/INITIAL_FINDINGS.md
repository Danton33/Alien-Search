Alien Search — Initial Findings
Starter research collection • November 2, 2026
This report opens a continuing investigation of government-provided records. It separates what a record documents, what its authors or witnesses claim, and what remains unverified. Source IDs resolve to the accompanying register, which records original links, saved copies, review scope and access gaps. The original review used 34 source entries and 14 PDFs totaling 394 pages. This portable repository supplies public locators and historical notes; it does not include those PDFs or imply they were re-reviewed for this release.

Framework and research rules
What the starter evidence supports
The records establish decades of official investigation, unresolved reports and specific disclosure and recordkeeping gaps. Several sources merit sustained case-level research. The reviewed material does not establish an extraterrestrial origin for a particular object or confirm intelligent visitors. This is a statement about the evidence reviewed, not a claim that all cases are explained or that the public record is complete.
Two questions should remain open independently: whether an observation is well documented and whether its origin is known. A classified source may protect a sensor rather than an object’s identity; a public report can be authentic while relaying unsupported information. Agency findings deserve inspection of their supporting data, just as witness claims do.
1. Current release trail: official 2026 records
NARA’s RG 615 page, reviewed October 27, 2026, lists agency series from FAA, NRC, ODNI, OSD, NSA, State, FBI, Air Force and CBP. It is a practical map for expanding the collection, not a statement that all relevant records have been transferred or digitized. [A002]
A Wayback capture of the official PURSUE page at 19:52:53 UTC on October 23, 2026 lists seven releases through that date. Release 07 is listed as a 325 MB document bundle with no videos. The page describes the released cases as unresolved, sometimes because of inadequate data. The static capture contains release links but does not populate the dynamic record database; its zero-file template counter is not a valid inventory total. The bundles have not been reviewed here. [A019]
Research implication: prioritize item-level acquisition and provenance before treating a new release as new evidence. A later release may re-publish an older record or provide context rather than a new incident. The archive capture verifies the portal’s contents at that time; it does not prove there were no later releases.
2. AARO FY2025: unresolved cases worth pursuing
The recovered official report has a May 30, 2025 information cutoff. It records 319 incoming reports: 284 events within the reporting period and 35 older events. It reports 114 resolutions within that cohort, nine reports for further analysis, 191 in active archive, three proposed closures and two merger actions. These are report/case-management categories, not independent counts of unusual objects. [A018, PDF pp. 3, 5–6]
One maritime report describes approximately 100 airborne UAP and two likely uncrewed surface systems off Virginia; AARO says investigation continued. Two reports describe possible aircraft electronic/avionic interference, with causation undetermined. Separately, the report lists 50 nuclear-site UAS incidents and explicitly says none were reported as UAP. [A018, PDF pp. 6 and 10]
Research implication: seek the original Virginia mission reports, sensor data, chronology and follow-up analysis. Preserve the distinction between narrative performance estimates and measured kinematics. A report’s “active archive” status means data were insufficient for a firm assessment; further-analysis status is a different investigative category.
3. Military observations: the 2021 baseline
ODNI’s June 2021 assessment covers a dataset primarily from November 2004 through March 2021: 144 reports, 80 involving multiple sensors. It describes 18 incidents, represented by 21 reports, with reported unusual movement or flight characteristics. One report was identified with high confidence as a deflating balloon; the others remained unexplained in that assessment. The report explicitly retains sensor errors, spoofing and observer misperception as alternatives requiring more analysis. [A009, PDF pp. 3–5]
Research implication: obtain incident-level data rather than interpreting the summary as 143 confirmed exotic craft. Multiple sensors can strengthen an observation, but independence, calibration, range estimation and common failure modes still matter. Historical status must not be represented as the current disposition of each case.
4. Annual-report counts require careful comparison
The FY2024 report describes 757 incoming reports, including 485 events within its window and 272 earlier events reported later. It reports 118 resolutions during the period and an additional 174 cases finalized by publication, all attributed to ordinary objects. It states that AARO had found no evidence of extraterrestrial beings, activity or technology at that time. [A010, PDF p. 3 / printed p. 2]
Research implication: incoming-report growth is affected by backlogs, reporting changes, observation coverage and case mergers. Do not add annual holdings, new reports and resolutions as if they were mutually exclusive incident counts. Do not extrapolate an extraterrestrial share from unresolved percentages.
5. Trans-en-Provence: a substantive trace-evidence dossier
GEIPAN retains the January 8, 1981 case as Class D. Its summary describes a single witness’s reported object, ground traces, gendarmerie sampling and later laboratory work on soil and vegetation. It also says the analyses did not prove the witness’s narrative or determine the phenomenon’s origin. This supports careful investigation without establishing a spacecraft landing. [A025]
The preserved 67-page GEPAN Note Technique No. 16, dated March 1, 1983, supplies the technical investigation, alongside an 11-page police scan. Its opening discussion identifies the potential value of comparing witness information with physical traces and the importance of collection conditions. The police scan requires image-level transcription before detailed claims can be adopted from it. [A026–A027]
Research implication: this is a high-priority dossier because there are inspectable measurements and sampling records. Review controls, site access, collection timing, weather exposure, potential contamination and the specific inference connecting any trace to the reported object. A real physical trace does not identify its cause by itself.
6. Valensole: an explicit encounter claim in an official archive
GEIPAN’s July 1, 1965 Valensole dossier records a farmer’s report of an unfamiliar craft, two figures and ground traces. It remains Class D. The page describes a single witness, later trampling of the site, and the absence of a comprehensive GEPAN investigation in the intervening period. The agency did not exist when the reported event occurred. The public webpage and one-page synthesis have been preserved or indexed; the much larger gendarmerie file still needs retrieval and review. [A028–A029]
Research implication: this directly matches the search for government-provided records about alleged beings, but its official hosting does not authenticate the encounter. Examine the earliest police statements, trace measurements and later retellings separately, preserving discrepancies and the elapsed time.
7. Congressional testimony: important claims with different foundations
The July 26, 2023 hearing includes Fravor’s account of the 2004 encounter and testimony from Graves and Grusch. When questioned about nonhuman biologics, Grusch attributed that assessment to people he said had direct program knowledge; supporting documentation was not presented for public examination in that exchange. The transcript records those statements under oath, without independently establishing a recovery program or origin. [A012, PDF pp. 14–17 and 44 / printed pp. 10–13 and 40]
Research implication: separate each witness’s direct observations from information learned from others. Record which supporting records are publicly available and which are only asserted to exist. Treat an unavailable classified answer as unavailable evidence, rather than evidence for a preferred explanation. The saved 2024 Senate opening statement is queued for detailed comparison. [A013]
8. Roswell: documented record gaps
GAO’s 1995 audit found surviving 1947 references and identified destroyed RAAF administrative records and outgoing messages. The disposition form did not identify who destroyed those records, when, or the governing authority. The audit also discusses ordinary records-management practices and surviving descriptions of balloon-related debris. The documented gap is significant for archival completeness, but it does not establish the motive for destruction or the nature of an alleged craft. [A004, PDF pp. 3–6]
Research implication: track missing series as a records problem with defined dates and custodians. Compare contemporaneous records with later Air Force investigations and testimony. An absence of preserved records is not interchangeable with proof of either a cover-up or a complete explanation.
9. Government secrecy is documented in some contexts
The CIA historical account discusses U-2/OXCART-related sightings and withholding their true cause to protect reconnaissance programs. It also describes sanitizing information about CIA involvement in the Robertson Panel. These are concrete reasons to inspect official communications critically; they are not evidence that the concealed activity was extraterrestrial. [A005, PDF pp. 7–8]
AARO’s 2024 historical review concludes that the investigations it reviewed did not confirm extraterrestrial technology. Its KONA BLUE section says the proposed DHS program was not established and received no transferred data or material. That is AARO’s published assessment, not an independent reproduction of every classified search it describes. The underlying KONA BLUE record set remains an acquisition priority. [A011, PDF pp. 7–10 and 34–35; A014]
10. Two traps in interpreting government collections
The FBI explains that the March 22, 1950 Hottel memo contains an uninvestigated second- or third-hand report. It says the memo does not prove a saucer recovery and gives no reason to connect it to Roswell. The date, source chain and actual investigative disposition matter more than the memo’s official letterhead. [A007–A008]
NSA’s “UFO and Other Paranormal Information” page lists search terms for which responsive material was not located. A dramatic term on that list is not the title of a matching secret document. Government websites also host third-party statements and clippings; inspect authorship before assigning authority. [A034]
11. Foreign records add methods and cases
GEIPAN’s methods separate identified cases (A), probably identified cases (B), insufficient-data cases (C) and unexplained-after-investigation cases (D). Its page dates the published-case statistics to March 31, 2026: 66.5% A/B, 30.3% C and 3.2% D. These are classifications within that published corpus, not probabilities that an observation is extraterrestrial. [A024]
Canada’s June 2025 Sky Canada report assesses fragmented public reporting and recommends more coordinated scientific handling. It explicitly focuses on reporting services rather than determining what UAP are. The UK archive guide and briefing supply routes into MoD files; Brazil’s Arquivo Nacional guide locates the official OVNI collection. Individual UK and Brazilian case files remain a next-stage task. [A022–A023, A030–A031]
12. Actual life-detection science belongs in its own lane
NASA’s September 2023 UAP study emphasizes better calibrated data, metadata, systematic analysis and reproducibility. Its scientific-context section says the peer-reviewed literature then contained no conclusive evidence of an extraterrestrial origin for UAP. It also treats the search for life and technosignatures beyond Earth as a legitimate scientific pursuit. [A032, PDF pp. 26–27 and 35]
NASA’s September 2025 announcement describes a potential biosignature in the Sapphire Canyon sample from Cheyava Falls on Mars. The mission context stresses that more data and study are needed before a life conclusion. A potential ancient microbial-life indicator does not support an inference about intelligent visitors to Earth. [A033]
Priority work queue
* PURSUE: download manageable document batches, inventory by source ID and event, and identify genuinely new primary records.
* Virginia maritime report: locate the original incident documentation and any subsequent case disposition.
* Trans-en-Provence: inspect all technical appendices, translate carefully and audit the sampling chain and controls.
* Valensole: obtain the original police file and compare initial statements with later additions.
* Nimitz and JAL 1628: link witness accounts to contemporaneous records and released sensor data, clearly recording missing material.
* Materials and video analysis: obtain the primary ORNL and Go Fast reports before adopting secondary descriptions of their conclusions.
Coverage boundary: this starter set preserves originals and reviews selected relevant sections. It does not claim every page, scan, release bundle or catalog series has been fully analyzed. Live access blocks, historical captures and pending transcription are identified in the register.


Focused question — Has a human ever shared a room with an alien?
Assessment dated November 2, 2026. Interpreting “alien” as an extraterrestrial being, including alleged recovered remains: the records reviewed do not establish a publicly verified instance of a human sharing a room with one. Whether an undisclosed event occurred is unknown. This finding does not claim to prove that such contact has never happened.
The API was used to read A012 (the 2023 congressional hearing), A011 (AARO’s historical review) and the preserved A018 snapshot of the FY2025 report. Exact source identity and capture provenance were retained. The archived report is historical evidence with its own information cutoff; it does not settle all later developments. New release bundles and all possible national archives have not been exhaustively reviewed.
A012 — David Grusch’s July 26, 2023 testimony: when asked whether he had seen any of the bodies, he answered, “That is something I have not witnessed myself.” His later account of nonhuman biologics attributed that assessment to people he said had direct program knowledge. This exchange does not supply firsthand observation by him or public independent verification of the alleged remains. Locators: physical PDF page 40 / printed page 36, and physical PDF page 44 / printed page 40.
A007 — The FBI describes the Hottel memo as an uninvestigated second- or third-hand claim. Its government provenance establishes that the claim was recorded, not that the reported beings or their extraterrestrial origin were authenticated.
A011 — AARO evaluated an account that aliens observed a military materials test. It judged the account most likely a misunderstanding involving a test unit’s nickname. That is the agency’s published assessment of this specific claim; it is not independent proof about every alleged encounter. Locator: physical PDF page 31 / printed page 31.
Evidence required to upgrade this assessment would include a sufficiently documented firsthand encounter and independently examinable evidence establishing extraterrestrial origin, such as authenticated biological material with a reliable custody history and independently reproduced analysis. A witness’s credentials, an oath, classification or the label “nonhuman” alone does not establish extraterrestrial origin.
